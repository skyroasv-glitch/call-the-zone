About

A daily ball-or-strike game built on real pitches

Every day you get five genuinely borderline pitches, rebuilt in 3D from real tracking data. You watch each one from behind the plate and call it. Then the game reveals two answers: what the rulebook zone says, and what the human umpire actually called that day.

You are scored against the rulebook. The interest lives in the pitches where the two disagree — and on a set chosen for closeness, that is roughly two of every five.

How a call is decided

One formula decides every call, and the same constants are used by the data pipeline and by the game:

strike ⟺ |plate_x| ≤ 0.708 + 0.121
and sz_bot − 0.121 ≤ plate_z ≤ sz_top + 0.121

0.708 ft is half of the 17-inch plate. 0.121 ft is a baseball's radius. Any part of the ball over any part of the zone is a strike, which is why every edge is padded by one radius. The top and bottom of the zone come from the batter's own stance, measured per pitch — that is why the box moves from hitter to hitter.

What you see is what is judged. The ball follows a path that lands exactly on the recorded crossing point, and the white box is drawn at the true plate width with the ball at its true size — so a ball that looks like it is touching the line really is a strike.

Why the umpire disagrees

The rulebook zone is a geometric box. A human behind the plate is judging a 95 mph pitch in a fraction of a second, from a crouch, with a catcher moving in front of them. Framing, count, and where the catcher receives the ball all shift real calls. The disagreements are not umpire errors so much as the gap between a measured box and a human judgement — which is the whole argument automated strike zones exist to settle.

The two modes

  • Today's Five — the daily puzzle. Everyone gets the same five pitches, and it turns over at your local midnight. Finishing it keeps your day streak alive.
  • Endless Zone — pitch after pitch, shuffled from the whole archive, with five seconds to call each one. The clock starts when the ball reaches the plate, never before, so it is timing your judgement rather than your hardware.

Controls

Space throws · B / S calls it · R replays · Enter advances · Esc returns to the menu

The camera stays behind the catcher, where an umpire stands. You can zoom along that sightline but not orbit off it: reading the ball against the plate from the side turns a judgement call into a geometry problem, and it is not a view anyone calling a real pitch ever gets.

Where the pitches come from

Pitches are drawn from public Statcast pitch tracking published through Baseball Savant. Only taken pitches are used — called strikes and balls — because a swing has no umpire call to argue with. From those, the game keeps pitches that sit close to an edge but not so close that the call is invisible, then aims for about two umpire-versus-rulebook disagreements per day.
